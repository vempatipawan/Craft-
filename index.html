Homepage and product page, desktop and mobile

A responsive prototype, not a picture of one.

This is built in HTML and CSS rather than presented as flat artboards, so you can open it on your own phone and use it. Tap the fulfilment tabs, change the quantity, watch the buy bar appear. That felt like the honest way to show you mobile-first work, since roughly three quarters of your visitors will judge it that way.

Every product, price, description and photograph is your own, pulled from craft-amsterdam.nl. Nothing here is placeholder text or stock imagery.

What I changed, and why

Eight decisions where I took your direction somewhere else.

  1. Pickup or delivery is chosen on the product page, not at checkout

    Your current checkout asks for a full delivery address at field five and only asks whether the customer wants delivery at field fourteen. Someone collecting croissants in the Pijp types an address they never needed. Moving that choice up here also lets the page answer the real question, which is when can I actually have it.

  2. The bake schedule is a reason to buy, not small print

    Ready Wednesday, order by Monday. Made to order rather than sitting on a shelf is a premium signal, so it sits next to the price instead of hiding in a policy page.

  3. Gluten free is stated as craft, never as a medical warning

    Free-from comes first as a quiet row of confirmations, then the sentence that a coeliac customer actually needs: no wheat flour comes through the door, so there is nothing to cross-contaminate. Allergen detail moves into the accordion. The product stays the hero.

  4. The quantity cap became guidance instead of an error

    Your shop caps most items at six. Rather than letting someone pick eight and be refused, the control stops at six and explains why: you bake what is ordered.

  5. A sticky buy bar on mobile

    It carries the price and the chosen fulfilment date, so the decision stays visible while someone reads the ingredients. On a phone that is the difference between a considered purchase and a lost one.

  6. More air than the reference, and fewer boxes

    I stripped several card outlines and let the photography sit against the paper. Your food is the most premium asset you own and it competes with any border you put near it.

  7. Real people on the craftsmanship band

    Gerry and Nilüfer, and the actual bakery. A story about hands and a stubborn idea is more convincing than another styled flat lay, and no competitor can copy it.

  8. Texture, scale and motion, used sparingly

    A fine grain sits over the whole site so it reads as paper rather than screen. Headlines rise a line at a time, the hero drifts slightly as you scroll, and the croissants run off the edge of the page as a rail you drag. Every one of those is there to make the food feel handled rather than photographed, and all of it switches off for anyone who has reduced motion turned on.
